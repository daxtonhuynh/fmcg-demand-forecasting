Forecasting demand honestly, then planning around the error

A first forecast scored an impressive 14% error, until an audit showed it was reading marketing spend that is booked after the sale. With the leak removed, the best model lands at 28% error per SKU, and sales history alone cannot do much better. So the project stops chasing accuracy and builds what a planner needs instead: forecast ranges that mean what they say, one set of numbers from SKU to company, and stock policies priced in a month-by-month simulation.

01 / The question

No model beats history. So plan around the error.

Leakage audit · Rolling-origin validation · Probabilistic forecasts · Inventory simulation

28.2%SKU-month error of the best honest forecast: each SKU's historical average
26.2%noise floor: the error left even with perfect knowledge of each SKU's 2025 average
7.1%error on the company total, where SKU noise cancels out

Error = WAPE, one month ahead, on every month of 2025 with a rolling origin.

The error is not a modelling failure. It is the demand.

Within each SKU, this month's demand says almost nothing about next month's: autocorrelation is about zero and the calendar month explains about 2% of the variation. A better algorithm cannot learn a pattern that is not there.

That changes the question. A planner still has to decide how much stock to hold, what each level of service costs, and which information would actually reduce the error. The second half of the project builds the system that answers those three questions.

The sales data also set the business context: $14.45M net revenue at a 22.9% margin, three quarters of it from B2B, with Beverages the largest category and the least profitable (14.0% margin).

03 / The forecast

Why the simplest forecast wins

+Commercial drivers add nothing once timing is honest. Lagged marketing, discount and price change the error by 0.5 points or less. Bigger discounts go with bigger orders because of volume-tier pricing, and promoted orders are the same size as the rest (about 213 units).
−Complexity costs accuracy. Ridge regression and gradient boosting are significantly worse than each SKU's historical average one month ahead. For the company total, last year's same month is best (6.6%) because December peaks every year.
{{chart_models}}
{{chart_leak}}

The leakage auditOne rule for every input

  • A feature is allowed only if its value is known when the forecast is made. Each input was checked against that rule before any model was trusted.
  • Marketing follows the sale. It is booked per order at a median 11% of gross sales, so it moves with the same month's units, not the next month's.
  • Discounts are volume pricing. The discount-to-order-size link is tiered pricing for big B2B orders, not promotional lift.
  • Planned prices are a scenario. A model with planned discount and price (28.8%) is reported as an upper bound, because realised discounts follow the order mix.

The calibration monitorContinuous evaluation, month by month

  • Each month, cumulative coverage of the 95% quantile is compared with a binomial alarm limit.
  • Normal ranges crossed the limit in {{alarm_month}}: more misses than sampling noise can explain.
  • Empirical ranges stayed above 96% all year. Scaling by SKU volatility added width, not accuracy.
  • The cheapest method depends on the lead time: Normal with a 1–2 month lead time, empirical with none.
  • In a live process the alarm is the trigger to switch method, without waiting for an annual review.

05 / What would reduce the error

Better information, not better algorithms

+Large orders are predictable to the business, not to history. Orders of {{large_thr}}+ units are {{large_orders_share}} of orders but {{large_units_share}} of units, and {{large_b2b}} are B2B: exactly what a CRM pipeline or a distributor's order calendar shows weeks ahead.
+Half the pipeline is enough to matter. Seeing 50% of large orders takes the SKU error to {{voi50_wape}}, frees {{voi50_stock}} of stock and saves {{voi50_cost}} a year. Seeing all of them reaches {{voi100_wape}} and saves {{voi100_cost}}.

Running an override processWhat the simulation says to measure

  • Log every override with a reason code, the statistical number, the final number and, later, the actual.
  • Judge by error and by cost. The informed planner gains {{fva_informed}} points and saves {{fva_informed_cost}} a year.
  • Re-size the buffer when information enters. The informed override leaves stock unchanged; the same pipeline built into a re-calibrated forecast frees {{voi50_stock}}.
  • Optimism and trend-chasing destroy value ({{fva_opt}} and {{fva_chaser}} points), and both cost money.
  • The planners are simulated. Real Forecast Value Added needs real overrides; the log is ready for them.

How each layer works

The technical choices behind the findings

Leakage audit

Allowed only if known in advance

Formula checks, driver timing and a feature-availability table per input. Marketing correlates with the same month's units (r = 0.81) and not the next month's (r = −0.01); promoted and non-promoted orders average the same size. Only lagged or planned values survive.

Validation

Rolling origin, the same rule for every model

Each model is refitted every month on data known at that point and tested on every month of 2025, one and three months ahead. Differences against the historical average carry bootstrap 95% intervals, so "worse" means significantly worse.

Forecast ranges

Quantiles from the model's own past errors

Relative errors pooled across SKUs, using only windows observed before the forecast origin. Three methods: the Normal formula, split-conformal (the ⌈(n+1)τ⌉-th error) and a volatility-scaled version, chosen by pinball loss on July to December 2024.

Hierarchy

Coherent from SKU to company

A summing matrix over 1 total, 5 categories, 17 brands and 30 SKUs. Reconciliation projects all 53 base forecasts onto ones that add up, with OLS, structural (WLS) or shrinkage-covariance (MinT) weights, against bottom-up and top-down.

Inventory

Periodic review, order-up-to, lost sales

Review monthly, order up to the τ-quantile of demand over lead time plus one month, lose unmet demand, carry stock over. A six-month warm-up absorbs the starting stock; only 2025 is scored. The same simulated units are priced under every cost assumption.

Information

The upper-bound value of a pipeline

Each order of {{large_thr}}+ units is visible with probability p (30 random draws), and ranges are re-calibrated for each scenario. Four planner archetypes adjust forecasts; Forecast Value Added is the error before minus the error after.

The assumptionsVaried in the sensitivity analysis

  • Not modelled: warehouses by region, ordering costs, minimum order quantities, capacity.

07 / What a planner can do

Six decisions the system supports

Each rests on the 2025 simulation. Dollar figures scale with the assumed costs; the rankings held across all 18 lead-time and cost combinations.

1Keep the simple forecast and rank models by cost. The historical average is cheapest in 54 of 54 configurations; the next-best forecasts add {{next_best_add_min}} to {{next_best_add_max}} a year.
2Set service targets per SKU. From {{cr_min}} for {{cr_min_name}} to {{cr_max}} for {{cr_max_name}}: {{saving_u95}} a year less cost and {{stock_cut_u95}} less stock than a uniform 95%.
3Check the ranges every month. Promised 95%, delivered {{cov95_normal}}; the monitor flags it in {{alarm_month}}. Switch method when it alarms.
4Build visibility of large B2B orders. The only lever that beats the history-only floor: up to {{voi100_stock_pct}} less stock and {{voi100_cost}} a year at full visibility.
5Record marketing as a plan and audit every input. Budgets set before the month are the only way to measure marketing's effect; the 14% forecast could never be reproduced in planning.
